Pros & Cons

Pros
  • ✓ No permanent installation required—the tension-based design grips doorframes without drilling, anchors, or wall damage, making it genuinely portable. I've repositioned mine between rooms multiple times without any deterioration to the frame or bar.
  • ✓ Heavy-duty construction handles bodyweight pull-ups plus weighted vests confidently.   • ✓ Short footprint design (the 'Short II' distinction matters here) fits standard interior doorways without protruding awkwardly into rooms. Unlike longer models, this doesn't look like industrial equipment taking over your bedroom.
  • ✓ Multiple grip positions—neutral, wide, and standard pull-up width—give you legitimate training variety without buying multiple bars. This is underrated; most doorway bars force you into one awkward angle.
Cons
  • ✗ Tension-based mounting means doorframe quality matters significantly. Older homes with warped or damaged frames can struggle to hold consistent pressure, and I've seen users resort to additional padding or shims to keep the bar stable.
  • ✗ Weight capacity tops out around 300 pounds with a safety buffer, which matters if you're planning advanced loaded pull-ups with 50+ pound vests. The bar itself is strong enough, but the frame-grip mechanism is the limiting factor.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will this damage my doorframe?

Not if your doorframe is in reasonable condition. The tension-based gripping system distributes pressure evenly, and the rubber pads protect against marks or dents. I've used this in rental apartments without issues, though landlords understandably get nervous. The key is ensuring the frame is straight and solid—warped frames create uneven pressure points that can cause damage.

What's the difference between the Short II and the original Matador?

The Short II has a narrower footprint, making it fit standard 32–36 inch doorways cleanly. The original Matador was longer and better suited for wider commercial-style frames. Unless you have an unusually wide doorway, the Short II is the right pick for residential spaces.

Can I do muscle-ups or advanced movements on this?

Yes, but with caveats. The bar itself is rigid enough for muscle-ups, but momentum-based movements put dynamic stress on the frame-gripping mechanism. Strict pull-ups and chin-ups are its bread-and-butter; if you're regularly kipping or doing explosive movement, consider whether your specific doorframe is solid enough to handle that intensity. Most residential frames handle it fine, but older or damaged frames might shift under dynamic load.
