Pros & Cons

Pros
  • ✓ Installation takes literally five minutes with a drill. No wall anchors, no permanent damage. I've moved mine twice between doorways, and each time it was faster than making coffee. Cons
  • ✗ Doorway pull-up bars depend entirely on your doorframe strength and design. This isn't a Rogue defect; it's the limitation of the doorway format itself. You need to honestly assess your frame before committing.

Anyone over 6 feet should measure their specific doorway clearance before purchasing, or you'll have a frustrating experience with bent knees on every rep.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will this damage my doorframe or apartment walls?

The Matador Lite uses a screw-based tension system rather than nails, so damage is minimal and completely reversible. I've installed and removed mine multiple times, and the only marks left behind are tiny screw holes—smaller than anchors from a typical picture frame. Renters can fill them with white toothpaste before move-out. This beats wall-mounted bars that require drilling into studs.

Can I do strict pull-ups and weighted pull-ups on this?

Yes, but with limits. The bar handled my bodyweight strict pull-ups and 25-pound weighted reps without issues. Beyond that, you're relying on doorframe integrity. I tested it with 45 pounds total resistance, and there was noticeable (but safe) flex. For serious strength training with heavy weights, this doorway bar isn't your answer—step up to a rack-mounted setup.

How much space do you actually need, and will it fit a standard doorway?

Standard doorways are 32-36 inches wide, and the Matador Lite adjusts from roughly 26-37 inches. Measure your specific doorway because some older homes have narrower openings. You'll also need at least 12 inches of vertical clearance between the bar and ceiling for comfortable hanging. Hallways and pantry doors often work better than main doorways where headroom is tighter.
