The whey-versus-plants argument used to be simple: whey won on amino acids, plant protein won on digestion, and everyone picked a side. In 2026 the gap is much narrower. Modern plant blends stack pea and rice protein to patch each other's amino acid holes, add digestive enzymes, and land within a few hundred milligrams of leucine per scoop of a standard whey concentrate. Meanwhile, whey has gotten cheaper per gram than almost anything else on the shelf.

Power Life High Impact Plant Protein

Power Life's plant protein is the Tony Horton–backed formula built around a pea and rice protein base plus ProHydrolase, an enzyme blend intended to break protein down into smaller peptides so less of it sits in your gut. That matters more than marketing-speak suggests: pea protein's biggest real-world complaint isn't amino acids, it's bloating and that chalky aftertaste. In practice, this is one of the smoother-mixing plant powders in a shaker — no blender required — and the vanilla and chocolate versions taste closer to a whey shake than most pea-based competitors. Each scoop delivers roughly 20g of complete plant protein along with added vitamins and minerals depending on the current formulation, so it's doing double duty as a light nutrition supplement, not just a protein hit. The trade-off is price: expect somewhere in the $3–4 per serving range at full retail on a ~14-serving tub, dropping meaningfully if you use the subscribe-and-save option. That's four to five times what a tub of bulk whey costs per gram. You're paying for the enzyme system, the flavor work, the dairy-free formulation and a brand that also sells matched digestive and energy products if you want a stack rather than one canister. Optimum Nutrition Gold Standard 100% Whey

This is the benchmark, and it's the benchmark for a reason. Around 24g of protein and ~5.5g of BCAAs including roughly 2.5g of leucine per scoop, with a 5 lb tub (74 servings) frequently selling for $70–90 — call it $0.95–1.20 per serving. Nothing in this comparison touches that value, and the whey isolate/concentrate blend is fast-digesting and extremely well studied for post-training muscle protein synthesis. It's also Informed Choice tested on many production runs, which matters if you're drug tested. The drawbacks are real, though: it contains dairy and soy lecithin, it's a concentrate blend rather than a pure isolate (so lactose-sensitive lifters can get gas and bloating), it has artificial sweeteners in most flavors, and there's no added enzyme support. If your gut tolerates dairy, ON is objectively the most protein per dollar here. If it doesn't, no price is low enough.

Transparent Labs 100% Grass-Fed Whey Isolate

The premium whey pick. Roughly 28g of protein per 32g scoop — about an 87% protein-by-weight ratio, which is unusually high — with almost no carbs or fat, no artificial sweeteners or dyes, grass-fed sourcing and full third-party testing with published certificates of analysis. Leucine content is strong, typically over 3g per scoop. At around $2 per serving ($60ish for 30 servings), it sits between ON and Power Life on cost. Because it's an isolate, most of the lactose is stripped out, so a lot of people who bloat on Gold Standard do fine on this. The honest negatives: stevia-sweetened flavors divide people, texture is thinner than a concentrate, and it's still dairy — no help if you're vegan or genuinely dairy-intolerant.

Orgain Organic Protein

Orgain is the plant protein you can grab at Costco or Target for roughly $1.30–1.80 per serving, which makes it the cheapest plant option in this lineup by a wide margin. You get around 21g of protein from a pea, brown rice and chia blend, USDA organic certification and no added sugar in the standard line. But the texture is noticeably grittier than Power Life's, the flavor is earthier, and the formula leans on plain plant isolates without the enzyme technology — so if pea protein bloats you, Orgain won't fix that. It also carries more carbs per serving than the whey options. As an everyday, throw-it-in-a-smoothie protein for general health, it's excellent value. As a targeted post-lift recovery tool for a serious lifter, it's the least optimized product on this list.

The leucine question, honestly

Here's the science that should shape your decision: muscle protein synthesis is triggered largely by leucine, and whey is denser in it. Pea/rice blends generally need roughly 20–30% more total protein to match a whey dose's anabolic signal — meaning a 40g plant serving to equal a 30g whey serving. Plant protein absolutely builds muscle; multiple 2020s trials show equivalent hypertrophy when total protein is matched high enough. So plant protein isn't inferior, it's just less efficient per gram, which compounds the cost difference. If you're using a plant powder as your primary post-workout protein, plan on a scoop and a half.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does plant protein really build as much muscle as whey?

Yes, when total protein and leucine are matched. Trials from the past several years show comparable strength and hypertrophy outcomes between pea-based and whey protein when daily protein intake is adequate. The catch is efficiency: plant blends are lower in leucine per gram, so you generally need 20–30% more per serving — think 40g of plant protein versus 30g of whey — to produce the same muscle protein synthesis response.

Why is Power Life so much more expensive per serving than Optimum Nutrition?

Three reasons. Plant protein isolates cost more to produce than whey, which is a dairy industry byproduct made at enormous scale. Power Life adds a ProHydrolase enzyme blend plus added micronutrients rather than selling plain protein. And ON sells in 5 lb bulk tubs (74 servings) while Power Life sells smaller tubs, which raises per-serving cost. The subscription price narrows the gap but doesn't close it.

Which of these proteins is safest if I'm drug tested in competition?

Look for third-party sport certification specifically. Optimum Nutrition Gold Standard runs Informed Choice testing on many batches, and Transparent Labs publishes certificates of analysis for each lot. If you compete in a tested sport, verify the certification on the exact tub and batch you buy — brand-level claims aren't enough, and this is one area where you shouldn't take marketing copy at face value.
