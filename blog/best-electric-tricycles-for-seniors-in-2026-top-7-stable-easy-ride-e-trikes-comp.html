Three wheels change everything. For riders in their 60s, 70s and beyond — or anyone managing balance issues, knee replacements or a bad hip — an electric tricycle removes the single scariest part of cycling: staying upright at low speed. But e-trikes are not interchangeable. A $999 direct-import trike and a $2,900 American-assembled fat-tire model can look similar in photos and ride nothing alike once you hit a cracked sidewalk or a 6% grade.

Mooncool TK1 / TK3 — Best Value for Comfort-First Riders

Mooncool has carved out the middle of the e-trike market: better spec'd than the $900 Amazon trikes, meaningfully cheaper than Addmotor or Emojo. The TK1 typically lands in the $1,299–$1,699 range depending on promotions, with a 500W–750W rear hub motor, 48V battery (roughly 20–48 miles claimed, realistically 20–30 with throttle use), a low step-through frame, and a rear differential — which matters more than most buyers realize. Cheap trikes drive only one rear wheel, which makes turns feel like they're fighting you; a differential lets both wheels turn at different speeds through a corner. Mooncool also ships with a large rear cargo basket standard, adjustable handlebars, and a wide padded saddle with backrest on several models. Where it stands out against Lectric is comfort and cargo out of the box; where it trails Addmotor is component quality — expect house-brand hydraulic or mechanical discs rather than Tektro/Shimano across the range. Support is US-based but smaller-scale, and there's no dealer network, so you're doing your own assembly or paying a local shop $75–$150. Check current Mooncool trike pricing and specs here.

Lectric XP Trike — Best for Small Spaces and Fastest Support

At around $1,499, the Lectric XP Trike is the folding option, and it's genuinely good at folding — it collapses small enough for an RV bay, a car trunk or a garage corner. It arrives 90% assembled in one box, which is a real advantage for seniors who don't want to wrestle with handlebars and torque specs. Lectric's 500W (1,092W peak) motor and 48V/14Ah battery are solid, and the company's customer service reputation is the best in the category — phone support, fast parts shipping, huge owner community. The tradeoffs: the folding hinges add flex you can feel on rough pavement, the 20"/16" wheel setup rides harsher than Mooncool's larger wheels, and the seat is comfortable but the stock basket is smaller. If you live in an apartment, travel with an RV, or want a brand you can call at 3pm and get a human, Lectric is the safer buy.

Addmotor Grandtan / Soletan — Best Build Quality (At a Price)

Addmotor sits at the premium end, typically $2,399–$3,199. The Grandtan M-340 and its siblings use fat 20"×4" tires, hydraulic disc brakes, torque sensors on some models, and Samsung or LG cells in larger 20Ah packs. The frames are noticeably beefier, the welds cleaner, and the ride quality on gravel or broken asphalt is in a different league from anything under $1,700. Addmotor also offers full-suspension and semi-recumbent trike variants that some riders with back problems strongly prefer. The catch is simply money: you're paying roughly double a Mooncool for maybe 30–40% more trike. If your rides are on smooth paved paths, that premium buys you durability and comfort margin you may never use. If you're riding rural roads, hills, or carrying weight regularly, it's justified.

Sixthreezero EVRYjourney Electric Trike — Best Ergonomics and Sizing Help

Sixthreezero's electric trike runs roughly $1,899–$2,299 and its differentiator is fit. The brand's upright, feet-forward geometry lets shorter riders put both feet flat on the ground while seated, and the company's online body-fit tool is unusually useful for buyers unsure about frame size. Components are mid-tier (500W hub motor, mechanical or hydraulic discs depending on year), and the range is modest at a realistic 20–25 miles. It's slower and less powerful than Mooncool or Addmotor, but for flat suburban neighborhoods and riders under 5'5", the ergonomics are hard to beat. Warranty is one year, similar to the field.

Emojo Caddy Pro — Best for Cargo and Load Capacity

The Caddy Pro, around $2,299–$2,599, is the errand-runner's trike. It carries a 400 lb total capacity, a huge rear basket, fat 24"/20" tires and a 500W geared motor with strong low-end pull. It's heavy — over 80 lbs — and that weight is a real issue if you ever need to lift or push it without power. But if your use case is groceries, farmers markets, and dog carriers, it beats Mooncool on sheer hauling ability and beats Lectric decisively. Dealer availability through independent bike shops is a plus for service.

Jorvik JET3 — Best UK-Style Comfort Trike

Jorvik built its reputation on adult trikes for mobility-limited riders, and the JET3 (roughly $1,900–$2,400 in the US) reflects that: very low step-over, upright seating, integrated rear rack, and a genuinely gentle power delivery that doesn't lurch. It's the most "mobility aid, not sports equipment" trike here, and for nervous riders that's a feature. Range and power are middling, and US parts availability is thinner than domestic brands — the main reason it doesn't rank higher for most American buyers.

Viribus Electric Trike — Best Budget Entry (With Caveats)

At $899–$1,099, Viribus is the cheapest credible option. You get 250W–500W, a 36V battery, a single-driven rear wheel (no differential on base models), mechanical brakes and heavier steel construction. It works for flat, short, occasional rides. It struggles on hills, the range claims are optimistic, and warranty support is largely email-based. If your budget genuinely caps at $1,000, it beats not riding at all — but the $300–$500 step up to Mooncool buys a differential, better braking and a real battery, and that's the single highest-value upgrade in this whole comparison.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are electric tricycles actually safer than two-wheeled e-bikes for seniors?

For balance and low-speed stability, yes — a trike won't tip over when you stop, mount or dismount, which is where most senior cycling injuries happen. But trikes handle differently in corners: they don't lean, so you must slow down before turns or you can lift a wheel. Practice in an empty parking lot for your first few rides regardless of brand.

How much should I expect to spend on a decent senior e-trike in 2026?

Below $1,100 (Viribus tier) you're usually giving up a rear differential and getting weak mechanical brakes. The $1,300–$1,700 band, where Mooncool and Lectric compete, is the value sweet spot. Above $2,300 you're buying premium components and heavier-duty frames from Addmotor and Emojo — worth it for hilly terrain or heavy cargo, overkill for flat paths.

Do these trikes come assembled, and can I do it myself?

Lectric ships roughly 90% assembled in a single box and is the easiest for a solo senior to finish. Mooncool, Addmotor and most others require attaching the rear axle assembly, handlebars, basket and pedals — doable with a helper and about 60–90 minutes, but many buyers pay a local bike shop $75–$150 to assemble and safety-check it, which is money well spent on brake and axle torque alone.
