Power Life High Impact Plant Protein

Power Life is Tony Horton's supplement line, and its plant protein leans hard into one specific problem: plant proteins that sit like cement in your gut. The formula is pea-protein-based and built around a digestive enzyme blend, so the pitch is essentially "plant protein you can actually drink daily without the bloat." You get roughly 20g of protein per scoop plus a full amino acid profile, and it's dairy-free, soy-free, and gluten-free. Where it's most useful is for people over 40 — Power Life's whole positioning is midlife fitness, and the enzyme support plus the plant base makes a lot of sense if you've noticed dairy hits differently than it did at 25. The honest downside is price: buying directly from Power Life, a single tub typically lands in the $40–$50 range depending on subscription status and bundles, which puts it well above the mass-market plant proteins per serving. It also isn't sold in most grocery stores, so you're ordering online, and the flavor lineup is narrow compared to the giants. Check current Power Life pricing and bundles here.

Optimum Nutrition Gold Standard 100% Whey

This is the benchmark, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. Gold Standard delivers 24g of protein per scoop with about 5.5g of BCAAs including roughly 2.4g of leucine — the amino acid that actually flips the muscle-protein-synthesis switch. It's whey protein isolate as the primary source, it mixes with a spoon in water, and it's Informed Choice tested for banned substances. Cost per serving usually runs around $1.00–$1.40 depending on tub size and where you buy, which is often half of what premium plant proteins cost. If your only goal is fall strength gains and you digest dairy fine, this is the most efficient dollar-for-gram choice on this list. The catch is the one everyone knows: it contains lactose (small amounts, but real), and a meaningful chunk of adults get gas, bloating, or worse from daily whey. There are no digestive enzymes, no prebiotics, and no fiber. It's a muscle tool, not a gut tool.

Orgain Organic Protein

Orgain is the price leader in plant protein and the easiest to find — it's in Costco, Target, and most grocery stores. You get 21g of protein per serving from a pea, brown rice, and chia blend, USDA Organic certification, and a cost per serving that frequently drops under $1.00 in the big-box tubs. That's genuinely hard to beat. The trade-offs are texture and formulation depth. Orgain has a grittier mouthfeel than most whey products, and several formulas use erythritol, which is itself a source of GI complaints for some people. There's no dedicated enzyme blend for digestion, and Orgain has faced consumer scrutiny over heavy metal content in plant proteins generally (a category-wide issue with pea and rice, not unique to them). If you want plant protein for budget and convenience reasons and your digestion is basically fine, Orgain is the sensible default.

Garden of Life Sport Organic Plant-Based Protein

This is the most feature-dense plant option here. Garden of Life Sport packs 30g of protein per serving — the highest on this list — from an organic pea, navy bean, lentil, and cranberry seed blend, plus 2g of BCAAs supplemented with additional muscle-recovery aminos. Crucially for this comparison, it includes a probiotic and enzyme blend (around 2 billion CFU) plus organic tart cherry and turmeric for recovery. It's NSF Certified for Sport and Informed Choice certified, which matters if you compete in anything tested. Price is the sticking point: it typically runs $1.60–$2.20 per serving, making it the most expensive here alongside Power Life. Taste is also polarizing — the bean-and-lentil base gives it an earthy note that fruit-flavored smoothies mask better than water does.

The Digestion Question, Answered Directly

Whey isolate is absorbed faster and delivers more leucine per gram than any plant blend, so on pure muscle-signaling efficiency it wins. But absorption speed doesn't matter if the protein never gets used comfortably. For people with lactose sensitivity, IBS, or general post-shake bloat, plant protein with enzyme support — Power Life or Garden of Life Sport — removes the variable entirely. Also worth noting for fall specifically: as training volume ramps up, total daily protein intake matters far more than the source. A powder you'll actually drink twice a day beats a technically superior powder you skip because it upsets your stomach.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does plant protein really build less muscle than whey?

Slightly less per gram, yes — plant proteins are lower in leucine, the amino acid that triggers muscle protein synthesis. But research consistently shows the gap closes or disappears when total daily protein intake is adequate. Practically, taking a slightly larger scoop of plant protein or adding 20-30g more total daily protein evens things out. Consistency matters more than source.

Is Power Life worth the price over Orgain if both are plant-based?

It depends on why you're buying plant protein. If you chose plants for ethical or dietary reasons and your digestion is normal, Orgain does the job for roughly half the cost per serving. If you switched from whey specifically because of bloating and stomach discomfort, Power Life's enzyme blend and smoother mixing profile are the features you're paying for, and Orgain doesn't offer them.

Can I take whey and plant protein together?

Yes, and plenty of people do — whey isolate post-workout for its fast absorption and leucine content, plant protein at other times of day for gut comfort and fiber. If dairy is a mild irritant rather than a hard no, this split approach lets you get whey's advantages without drinking it three times a day.
