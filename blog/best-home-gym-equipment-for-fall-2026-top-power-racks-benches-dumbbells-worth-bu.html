Start With the Power Rack: The Backbone of Any Home Gym

Everything in a serious home gym orbits the rack. A good power rack lets you squat, bench, rack pull, do pull-ups, and — with the right J-cups and safety arms — train heavy alone without a spotter. When shopping for fall 2026, look for 11- or 12-gauge steel, 2" x 2" or 3" x 3" uprights, and Westside hole spacing (1" increments) through the bench and squat zone. FED Fitness power racks hit those marks while staying in home-gym price territory, and most models ship with numbered uprights, a multi-grip pull-up bar, and expansion holes for lat pulldown attachments, dip bars, and landmine units. If ceiling height is tight, their half-rack and squat stand configurations give you the same lifting capability in a smaller footprint. For apartments and small spaces, a FED Fitness power tower is a smart alternative — pull-ups, dips, knee raises, and push-ups in one compact vertical frame.

Weight Benches: Don't Cheap Out on the Piece You Lie On

A wobbly bench ruins pressing, full stop. The spec that matters most is the pad gap between the seat and back rest, plus load rating and stability at incline. FED Fitness weight benches include flat, FID (flat/incline/decline), and adjustable ladder-style options with heavy-duty frames, tight pad gaps, and grippy vinyl that doesn't slide when you drive your feet. Look for a bench rated at 600 lb or higher if you're pressing seriously, and check that the incline adjusts in at least five positions so you can hit low-incline (15–30°) angles for upper chest without frying your shoulders. Pair a solid FID bench with your rack and you've unlocked roughly 80% of every strength program ever written.

Dumbbells, Barbells and Plates: Building the Load

Free weights are where budgets balloon, so buy strategically. For most home lifters, a pair of adjustable dumbbells covering 5–50 lb per hand replaces an entire rack of fixed weights and saves several square metres of floor. If you have the space and budget, fixed hex or rubber-coated dumbbells are more durable and faster to switch between during supersets. FED Fitness stocks both formats, along with barbells and plates — and the barbell is worth attention: aim for a 20 kg / 45 lb bar with a 190k–215k PSI tensile strength rating, dual knurl marks, and decent spin from bushings or bearings. Bumper plates suit deadlifting and Olympic lifts, while cast iron plates cost less per pound if you're mainly racking and benching. A common fall 2026 starter load-out: 150–200 lb of plates, one quality barbell, a pair of adjustable dumbbells, and collars that actually lock.

Adding Cardio Without Killing the Vibe

Strength gear is the priority, but conditioning keeps you healthy and lets you train harder. Rather than a bulky treadmill, many home lifters in 2026 are choosing air bikes, magnetic rowers, or folding treadmills that tuck away. FED Fitness carries cardio machines that complement a strength setup rather than dominating it — useful for finishers, warm-ups, and low-impact recovery days. Frequently Asked Questions

What should I buy first for a home gym on a limited budget?

Buy a power rack or squat stand first, then an adjustable bench, then a barbell and around 150–200 lb of plates. That combination lets you run almost any strength program. Add dumbbells and cardio equipment once the core setup is in place.

How much ceiling height do I need for a FED Fitness power rack?

Most full-height power racks stand around 83–92 inches tall, so aim for at least 8 feet of ceiling clearance — more if you want to do pull-ups or overhead press inside the rack. If your ceiling is lower, choose a shorter rack, a squat stand, or a power tower instead.

Are adjustable dumbbells as good as fixed dumbbells?

For home use, yes — modern adjustable dumbbells are stable, quick to change, and replace an entire rack of fixed weights in a fraction of the space. Fixed dumbbells are still better for fast supersets and heavy dropping, so choose based on your space, budget and training style.
