Burchda Bikes (RX20 Max, RX80, RX70): the spec-per-dollar leader

Burchda's pitch is simple: more watts and more amp-hours for less money. The RX20 Max folding fat tire model typically lands in the $999–$1,299 range with a 750W rear hub motor, 48V 15Ah+ battery, 20" x 4.0" fat tires, front suspension and a color LCD display — a spec sheet that would cost $1,600+ from a US-based brand. The RX80 and RX70 push into 1000W-class motors with 26" x 4" tires for genuine sand and snow work. Where Burchda cuts corners is the parts you can't see on a spec sheet: cadence sensors rather than torque sensors on most models (so power arrives in a surge rather than proportionally), Shimano Tourney-level drivetrains, and mechanical or entry hydraulic disc brakes depending on the model. Assembly quality is generally good but you're the mechanic — there's no local shop network, and warranty claims run through email with parts shipped to you. If you're comfortable with a torque wrench and want the most hardware for your budget, this is the value play. Check current Burchda pricing and models here.

Lectric eBikes (XP 4, XP Lite 2.0): the folding commuter benchmark

Lectric is the brand Burchda is chasing. The XP 4 sells around $999 with free shipping, ships about 90% assembled in a single box, and includes a UL 2849-certified electrical system and UL 2271-certified battery — certification Burchda does not consistently document across its range, which matters if you live in an apartment building or a city (like New York) with battery rules. Lectric's 750W (1,310W peak) motor and hydraulic brakes on the XP 4 are competitive, and the XP Lite 2.0 at roughly $799 is the lightest practical folder here at about 46 lbs, versus 65–75 lbs for most Burchda fat tire folders. Lectric's real edge is scale: millions of units sold, a US call center, a huge accessory ecosystem and a mobile-mechanic network in many metros. Its weakness is tire volume and suspension — Lectric folders are commuter-first, and on rough gravel they get harsher than a 4-inch Burchda fat tire setup.

Rad Power Bikes (RadExpand 5, RadRunner Plus): pay more, worry less

Rad sits a tier above on price — the RadExpand 5 folding fat tire bike is typically $1,299–$1,499, and the RadRunner Plus utility model runs higher still — with lower headline specs (750W, 48V 14Ah, cadence sensor, mechanical discs on some builds). What you're buying is infrastructure: a network of Rad-authorized service partners and mobile mechanics across the US and Canada, genuinely good spare-parts availability years after purchase, UL-certified batteries, and a support team that answers the phone. For a rider who has never turned a wrench, that gap is worth the $300–$400 premium over Burchda. Rad also builds cargo-capable geometry and rack systems that are far better documented than most value-brand accessories, so if you plan to haul kids or groceries, it's the safer platform.

Aventon Sinch.2: the ride-quality pick among folders

At roughly $1,399–$1,599, the Sinch.2 is the most expensive folder in this group and the best-riding one. It's the only bike here with a torque sensor as standard, which transforms how the assist feels — power scales with your pedal effort instead of switching on in a block, making it smoother in traffic and more efficient on hills. You also get hydraulic disc brakes, a 500W (750W peak) motor, integrated lighting, a well-built app with ride tracking and over-the-air firmware, and a nationwide dealer network for service. The trade-off is raw output: Aventon's 500W nominal motor is less punchy from a stop than Burchda's 750W–1000W hub motors, and the battery capacity is similar rather than larger. You're paying for refinement and support, not for numbers.

Head-to-head: how they actually stack up

On price-to-spec, Burchda wins outright — nobody else gives you 1000W-class fat tire hardware near $1,200. On safety certification and resale confidence, Lectric and Rad win because their UL documentation is clear and consistent. On ride feel and control, Aventon wins on the strength of torque sensing and hydraulic brakes. On serviceability, Rad wins, followed by Aventon (dealers), then Lectric (mobile mechanics), then Burchda (DIY plus shipped parts). Weight is worth flagging too: any 4-inch fat tire folder, Burchda or Rad, is a 65–75 lb object. If you'll be lifting it into an SUV or up stairs regularly, the folding-fat-tire concept fights itself, and a Lectric XP Lite 2.0 is the honest answer.

Our Verdict

There's no single winner here, only best fits. Buy Burchda if you want maximum motor, battery and tire for the money, you're comfortable assembling and maintaining your own bike, and you'll ride mixed surfaces — gravel paths, beach, packed snow — where 4-inch tires genuinely pay off. Buy Lectric if your priority is a certified, no-drama folding commuter at the lowest total cost, or if you need something light enough to lift (XP Lite 2.0). Buy Rad Power if you've never wrenched on a bike and want a shop or mobile mechanic to handle everything, and buy the Aventon Sinch.2 if you'll ride daily and care more about how the assist feels than how many watts it claims. As for folding vs. fat tire within Burchda's own lineup: choose the folding RX20 Max if you have limited storage, a car trunk or an RV; choose the full-size RX80 or RX70 if the bike lives in a garage and you want the stability, range and comfort of a bigger frame and 26-inch wheels.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are Burchda e-bikes UL certified?

Burchda does not document UL 2849 (bike system) and UL 2271 (battery) certification as consistently across its lineup as Lectric or Rad Power do. If you live in a building or city that requires UL-certified batteries — New York City being the strictest example — confirm certification for the specific model in writing before buying, or choose a brand that publishes it clearly.

Is a folding fat tire e-bike actually practical?

It depends on how you fold it. Folded fat tire bikes like the Burchda RX20 Max or RadExpand 5 fit in a large trunk, RV bay or apartment corner, but they weigh 65–75 lbs, so lifting them regularly is hard work. If you need to carry the bike up stairs or onto a train, a lighter commuter folder such as the Lectric XP Lite 2.0 at roughly 46 lbs is the more realistic choice.

Does a 1000W Burchda motor beat Aventon's 500W torque-sensor motor?

For raw acceleration and steep-hill grunt, yes — higher nominal wattage and a punchy cadence-sensor hub motor will feel faster off the line. For smooth, efficient, controlled riding in traffic, the torque-sensing Aventon feels better because power scales with your pedal pressure. Pick based on whether you want thrust or finesse.
