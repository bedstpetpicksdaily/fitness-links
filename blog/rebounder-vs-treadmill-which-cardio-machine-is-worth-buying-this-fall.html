Rebounder vs. Treadmill: Which Cardio Machine Is Worth Buying This Fall? Boogie Bounce

By Best Fitness Picks Daily • September 02, 2026 • Contains affiliate links

Autumn is when home cardio equipment sales spike — the evenings get dark, outdoor running loses its charm, and suddenly that corner of the spare room looks like a gym. The two contenders most people land on are a rebounder (mini trampoline) or a treadmill. They're both cardio machines, but they solve genuinely different problems, and the price gap between them is enormous: a good rebounder bundle sits somewhere in the £150–£400 range, while a treadmill worth owning long-term usually starts around £700 and climbs past £2,000.

This comparison looks at Boogie Bounce alongside two other serious rebounder brands — JumpSport and bellicon — and two treadmills that regularly top buyer guides, the Sole F63 and NordicTrack's Commercial series. The aim isn't to crown one machine the universal winner. It's to work out which one fits your floor space, your joints, your budget and, honestly, the workouts you'll actually do in January when motivation dips.

Short version: if you want structured, choreographed, low-impact cardio in a small flat, a rebounder makes far more sense. If you're training for a half marathon or you need precise pace and incline data, a rebounder can't replace a treadmill. Below is the detail behind that.

Boogie Bounce — the choreographed rebounder built around classes

Boogie Bounce is a UK brand that grew out of the studio class scene rather than the equipment industry, and that shows in the product. Their rebounders are sold as bundles: a bungee-sprung or spring mini trampoline (typically 40in / 102cm depending on model), a stabilising handlebar, and access to their on-demand workout library, which is largely music-led, choreographed routines rather than generic "jog on the spot" content. Prices generally sit in the £200–£400 bracket depending on model and bundle, which puts a full cardio setup at roughly a quarter of the cost of a mid-range treadmill. The handlebar is the differentiator versus most rebounder brands — it's genuinely useful if you have balance concerns, are returning from injury, or are over 50 and want a confidence rail. The trade-off is that the handlebar makes the unit less compact when assembled, and the folding process is more fiddly than a bare-frame rebounder. Build quality is solid consumer-grade rather than commercial-gym-grade; expect several years of regular home use, not a decade of daily studio abuse.

JumpSport 250i / 350i — the quieter, more technical rebounder

JumpSport is the American benchmark for fitness rebounders and is the brand most often recommended by physios in the US. The 250i typically lands around $270–$300 and the 370 higher again. What you're paying for is the patented EnduroLast elastic cord system — no metal springs — which is noticeably quieter and gives a softer, deeper bounce than most spring units. JumpSport also lets you adjust cord tension on some models, so a 60kg user and a 100kg user can both get an appropriate bounce depth. Where it falls behind Boogie Bounce for UK buyers: import duties and shipping can add significantly to the sticker price, the included workout content is more functional than fun, and the standard models don't ship with a stability bar (it's a paid add-on). If you want the best pure bounce feel and you don't need choreography or a handlebar, JumpSport is arguably the better engineered product.

bellicon — the premium, buy-it-once rebounder

bellicon is the luxury end of this category, with prices commonly running £500–£900+ once you've chosen your mat size, bungee strength and leg type. The German-made bungee system is the softest bounce available and the frames come with long warranties (often 5 years on the frame, and the bungees are individually replaceable rather than requiring a whole new unit). For someone with joint pain, lymphatic health goals, or a plan to bounce daily for the next decade, bellicon is the rational purchase — the cost-per-year works out well. For someone who wants to try rebounding for the first time this autumn, spending bellicon money before you know whether you enjoy it is hard to justify. Boogie Bounce and JumpSport are the sensible entry points; bellicon is the upgrade you buy in year three.

Sole F63 — the treadmill that actually lasts

If you decide you genuinely need to run indoors, the Sole F63 is the perennial value recommendation, usually £900–£1,200 depending on sale. It has a 3.0 CHP motor, a 20" x 60" belt (long enough for tall runners), 15 levels of incline and a cushioned deck. It's heavy, it's plain, and its screen is basic — but it's the machine that survives. What no treadmill can escape is footprint and noise: even folded, the F63 occupies a significant chunk of a room, it weighs over 100kg, and running on it in an upstairs flat will not endear you to neighbours. A rebounder, by contrast, tucks behind a door and produces almost no impact noise through the floor.

NordicTrack Commercial 1750 — the treadmill for people who want the classes

The NordicTrack Commercial 1750 (typically £1,800–£2,300) is the closest treadmill equivalent to what Boogie Bounce is doing with content: a large touchscreen, iFIT trainer-led sessions, and automatic incline/decline and speed adjustment that follows the instructor. It's genuinely engaging and the -3% decline is rare at this price. But there are two costs people underestimate: iFIT is a subscription (roughly £15/month, and much of the machine's appeal evaporates without it), and the machine's complexity means more electronics to fail out of warranty. Compare that to a rebounder, which has no motor, no console and essentially nothing to break except the bungees or springs — which are cheap and user-replaceable.

The honest fitness comparison

Calorie-wise, the two are closer than people expect: moderate rebounding sits roughly in the 7–10 MET range, similar to a brisk 6mph jog, while hard treadmill running goes far higher. Rebounding wins decisively on joint load — the mat absorbs a large share of impact force that your knees and hips would otherwise take — and it trains balance, ankle stability and core control in a way treadmill running doesn't. Treadmills win on measurable progression, running specificity, and the ability to train genuinely hard at the top end. Neither is "better cardio"; they're different tools.

Our Verdict

Buy a treadmill if you're a runner. If you have a race in the diary, need to hold a specific pace, or want incline training, the Sole F63 (durability, value) or NordicTrack Commercial 1750 (guided classes, auto-adjusting incline) are the right purchases — accept the space, the noise and the £900+ outlay as the price of that specificity. Buy a rebounder if your goal is consistent, joint-friendly cardio in a small home, and Boogie Bounce is the strongest first rebounder for UK buyers thanks to the included handlebar, the class library and the sub-£400 entry point. Choose JumpSport instead if you're an experienced bouncer who cares more about cord quality and adjustable tension than choreography, and choose bellicon if you already know you'll rebound daily for years and want the last unit you'll ever buy. The genuinely wrong move this autumn is spending £2,000 on a treadmill you'll use twice a week for six weeks — a £300 rebounder you actually bounce on beats it every time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does rebounding burn as many calories as running on a treadmill?

At moderate intensity, yes — rebounding sits around 7–10 METs, comparable to jogging at roughly 6mph. But a treadmill lets you push far harder at the top end with sprints and steep inclines, so a serious runner will out-burn a rebounder session. For general fitness and fat loss, the difference is small enough that adherence matters more than the machine.

Is a Boogie Bounce rebounder safe if I have knee or back problems?

Rebounding is generally much lower impact than treadmill running because the mat absorbs a large share of the landing force, and the included stability handlebar helps if balance is a concern. That said, bouncing still loads the spine and pelvic floor, so check with a physio first if you have disc issues, and consider a bungee-based unit like bellicon or JumpSport if you need the very softest possible bounce.

How long do rebounder springs or bungees last compared to a treadmill belt?

Expect a few years of regular use before springs or cords lose tension — they're inexpensive and usually user-replaceable, which is a real advantage over treadmills. bellicon's individually swappable bungees last longest. Treadmill belts and decks typically need attention after several years of running, and motor or console failures out of warranty can cost hundreds to repair.
