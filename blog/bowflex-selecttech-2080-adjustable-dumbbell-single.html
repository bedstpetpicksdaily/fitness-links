Pros & Cons

Pros
  • ✓ The dial-based weight adjustment mechanism is genuinely fast—switching between 5 and 80 pounds takes roughly 3 seconds once you develop the muscle memory. No sliding collars, no pin systems that get sticky. This matters more than you'd think during supersets or drop sets when every second of dead time adds up.
  • ✓ Space efficiency is legitimate. One dumbbell occupies roughly the footprint of a shoe box when resting, compared to a full rack of traditional dumbbells that devours entire corners of your gym. For apartments or small rooms, this isn't marketing fluff—it's architectural necessity.
  • ✓ The 5-pound increment jumps are granular enough for progressive overload without being so granular that you're buying 15 different weights. Real lifters appreciate the precision between 30 and 50 pounds where jumps matter most.
  • ✓ Build quality justifies the premium. The handle diameter stays consistent across all weight ranges, and the metal construction doesn't flex or creak like some cheaper adjustable alternatives. After 3 years of consistent use, the SelectTech still feels solid.
Cons
  • ✗ The price-per-pound ratio breaks down hard when you actually do the math. At $300+ for a single 80-pound adjustable dumbbell, you're paying roughly $3.75 per pound. Buy two traditional dumbbells at 40 pounds each, and you're looking at $1.50-$2 per pound. That's a massive premium you're absorbing for convenience alone.
  • ✗ You'll need to buy the stand separately (another $100-150), which Bowflex conveniently doesn't emphasize. Without it, the dumbbell sits loose on your floor, and honestly, managing it becomes annoying. That brings your real entry cost to $400-450 for the complete system—closer to purchasing a solid mid-range adjustable dumbbell from a direct-to-consumer brand.

Our Verdict

The Bowflex SelectTech 2080 is genuinely a good dumbbell, but 'good' and 'worth the money' occupy different zip codes. Buy it if: you have zero floor space, you're committed to the Bowflex ecosystem and plan to stack multiple units, or you live in a place where traditional dumbbells genuinely aren't an option. Skip it if: you've got even a modest corner to dedicate to your gym, you're on a tight budget, or you're starting completely from scratch. At $300+ for a single weight, you're paying for convenience and compactness—not performance. Those are legitimate needs, but they're not everybody's needs. Compare this directly against Nuobell, PowerBlocks, or even traditional dumbbells before committing; the 4.3-star rating reflects a solid product, but solid doesn't always equal the smartest financial decision for your specific situation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I really need to buy the Bowflex stand separately?

Technically no, but practically yes. The dumbbell works without it, but managing a 80-pound weight sitting loose on your floor creates real safety and convenience problems. The stand runs $100-150 and isn't included with the dumbbell purchase. Factor this into your total cost calculation upfront—it's the difference between a $300 purchase and a $400+ commitment.

How does the SelectTech 2080 compare to PowerBlocks or Nuobells?

All three are adjustable systems, but they solve different problems. SelectTech uses a dial mechanism and weighs more (about 3.5 pounds per unit). PowerBlocks use a pin system and feel lighter but require more space per weight increment. Nuobells split the difference with a more compact footprint. SelectTech typically costs more, but the dial mechanism is faster once you're comfortable with it. Test all three if possible; the 'best' one depends entirely on your workflow and space constraints.

Are 5-pound increments enough for progressive overload?

For most lifters, yes. The real progression happens in the 30-60 pound range where 5-pound jumps feel meaningful. Below 30 pounds, you might wish for 2.5-pound precision. Above 70 pounds, most programs don't require micro-loading. The increments work well for compound movements; if you're doing advanced bodybuilding splits with lots of isolation work, you might hit limitations sooner.

How long do these typically last, and is the warranty good?

The mechanism is mechanically simple enough that durability issues are rare. The dial itself is the most likely component to wear, and it holds up well under normal residential use. Bowflex covers defects for 1-2 years depending on your purchase type, which is pretty standard for this category. Real-world reports show these lasting 5+ years without issues when maintained properly. That said, longevity doesn't completely solve the value equation when compared to indestructible traditional iron.
