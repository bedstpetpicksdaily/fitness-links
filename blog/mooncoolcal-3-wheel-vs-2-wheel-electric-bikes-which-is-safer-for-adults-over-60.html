If you're over 60 and shopping for an electric bike, the safety question usually comes down to one thing: how confident do you feel at a dead stop? Two-wheel e-bikes are lighter, faster and easier to store — but they demand you plant a foot and hold up 60+ pounds of bike every time you pause at an intersection. Three-wheel electric trikes never fall over, but they behave differently in corners and won't fit through a narrow garage door. Neither is universally "safer." They're safer for different people.

Mooncool (MC-3, TK1 and Fat-Tire Trikes)

Mooncool is the most trike-focused brand of the group — it isn't an e-bike company that added a trike, it's a company whose identity is adult tricycles. That focus shows up in the details that matter to older riders: very low step-through frames (roughly 13–16 inches on most models, so you step into the bike rather than swinging a leg over), wide padded saddles with backrests on several models, upright handlebars that don't force you to lean forward onto arthritic wrists, and large rear cargo baskets sized for actual grocery runs rather than a jacket. Most Mooncool trikes run a 500W–750W rear hub motor with a 48V battery, hydraulic or mechanical disc brakes depending on the model, and fat 20" x 4" tires on the off-road variants that absorb cracked sidewalks and gravel driveways. Pricing typically lands in the $1,299–$1,999 band, which is competitive with Lectric and clearly undercuts Rad's list price. The trade-offs: Mooncool has a smaller dealer and service footprint than Rad or Aventon, so most repairs are DIY or local-shop, and the trikes are heavy — expect 75–90 lbs, which makes car-rack transport impractical without a hitch platform rated for it. You can see the current lineup at Mooncool's site.

Lectric XP Trike

The XP Trike, priced around $1,499 and frequently bundled with accessories, is the value benchmark in this category and the trike most likely to be a Mooncool cross-shop. Its headline trick is that it folds — genuinely useful if you're storing it in an apartment or a shared garage, and something no Mooncool model matches. It ships with a 500W motor (peak higher), hydraulic disc brakes as standard, a rear differential, and a step-through height of about 11 inches, the lowest here. Lectric's customer service reputation is strong and parts are easy to get. Where it falls short against Mooncool: the folding mechanism adds joints and complexity to a frame that carries a lot of load, the standard 20"/16" wheel setup rides harsher than Mooncool's fat-tire trikes on rough pavement, and the stock cargo basket is smaller than what Mooncool includes on several models. If storage space is your binding constraint, Lectric wins outright.

Rad Power Bikes RadTrike

The RadTrike is the premium, most conservatively engineered trike of the three. List price has hovered near $2,199 (it discounts to the $1,500s during sales), and you're paying for Rad's service network — hundreds of authorized mobile mechanics and shops in the U.S., which is a legitimate safety feature when you're 68 and don't want to adjust your own brake calipers. It uses a 750W geared hub motor, a low 11-inch step-through, and a very compact 63-inch overall length that makes it the easiest of the trikes to maneuver in a bike lane. Downsides versus Mooncool: it's capped at 14 mph, which some riders find genuinely too slow for keeping up with traffic on a shared road, the battery capacity is modest so real-world range often lands in the 20–30 mile zone, and at full price you're paying several hundred dollars more than a comparably specced Mooncool for the service network rather than the hardware.

Aventon Pace 500.3 (2-Wheel Step-Through)

If you have good balance and no hip or knee limitations, a two-wheel step-through like the Pace 500.3 (around $1,599) is faster, lighter (about 62 lbs), corners intuitively and fits on a standard car rack — none of which is true of any trike here. It has a torque sensor, which means the pedal assist responds to how hard you pedal instead of just switching on, and that makes low-speed starts smoother and more predictable than the cadence-sensor systems on most trikes. But it still requires you to balance from a stop, and 62 lbs falling sideways at a red light is exactly the scenario that sends riders over 60 to urgent care. Choose it only if you're currently riding a regular bike without hesitation.

Lectric XP 3.0 (2-Wheel Folding)

At roughly $999, the XP 3.0 is the cheapest credible entry point and America's best-selling e-bike for good reason. It folds, it has fat 3" tires that add stability, and the step-through version has a low standover. For seniors who want a bike mainly for flat rail-trails and short errands and who still feel steady on two wheels, it's hard to beat on price. The caveats are real, though: it's 64 lbs of folding bike, the 20" wheels make it twitchy at speed compared with the Aventon, and the cadence sensor's on/off power delivery can surprise a nervous rider on the first few outings. It is not the right choice for anyone with balance issues, vertigo, or a history of falls.

The Safety Data Nobody Prints on the Box

Trikes eliminate the most common senior e-bike injury — the low-speed tip-over at a stop — but they introduce a different one: lateral instability in corners. A tadpole or delta trike taken too fast through a turn, especially with a loaded rear basket, can lift a wheel. All three trikes here are delta-style (one wheel front, two rear) and all require you to slow down noticeably before turning. Trikes are also wider than a standard bike lane in some cities and won't fit through many park bollards. Two-wheelers avoid all of that but require balance you may not have in five years. Buy for the rider you'll be at 70, not the one you were at 50.

Our Verdict

There's no single winner here — there's a right answer per rider. If you have any balance concerns, a history of falls, hip or knee limitations, or you simply want to stop worrying about tipping at intersections, buy a trike, and among trikes Mooncool offers the best hardware-per-dollar with the most senior-specific ergonomics; browse the range at Mooncool. If storage space is your hard constraint, the folding Lectric XP Trike beats Mooncool outright and costs about the same. If you want a mechanic within driving distance and you'll pay for that peace of mind, the RadTrike is worth the premium despite its 14 mph ceiling and modest range. And if you still ride a regular bicycle today without a second thought, don't buy a trike at all — the Aventon Pace 500.3's torque sensor and 62 lb weight make it a better, faster, more portable machine, and the $999 Lectric XP 3.0 is the budget pick. Be honest about your balance today, and buy one step more stable than you think you need.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are electric trikes actually safer than two-wheel e-bikes for seniors?

For stop-and-start stability, yes — a trike can't tip over when you stop, which eliminates the most common low-speed fall. But trikes can lift a wheel in fast corners, especially with a loaded rear basket, and they're wider than bike lanes in some areas. They're safer for riders with balance concerns; they're not automatically safer in every situation.

How does Mooncool compare on price to Lectric and Rad Power?

At the time of writing, Mooncool trikes generally run about $1,299–$1,999, the Lectric XP Trike sits near $1,499, and the RadTrike lists around $2,199 but discounts frequently. Mooncool usually offers more motor and battery for the money than Rad at list price; Lectric competes closely and adds folding.

Can I put an electric trike on a car rack?

Rarely. Most adult e-trikes weigh 75–95 lbs and are 32–35 inches wide, which exceeds the capacity and geometry of standard hitch racks. You'll typically need a heavy-duty platform rack rated for trikes, a ramp and a van or truck, or a small utility trailer. This is one of the few areas where two-wheel e-bikes like the Aventon Pace 500.3 have a clear practical edge.
