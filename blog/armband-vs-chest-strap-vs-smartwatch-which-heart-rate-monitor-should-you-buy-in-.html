Heart rate training only works if the number on your screen is actually correct. They are not interchangeable — they measure different things, in different places, with meaningfully different error rates, and they cost anywhere from "already in your pocket" to $400+.

Ninja Play Fitness Heart Rate Armband — comfort and adaptive zones for functional training

Ninja Play Fitness sits in the optical armband category, which is the sensible middle ground: worn on the upper forearm or bicep, it reads from tissue that moves far less than the wrist, avoiding the two big failure modes of a smartwatch (tendon movement during gripping, and the sensor lifting off during flexion). Its differentiator versus a plain Verity Sense is software: the platform builds adaptive training zones that recalibrate as your fitness changes, rather than using the static 220-minus-age formula that still powers most cheap apps. For circuit and functional training — kettlebells, sled pushes, EMOMs, ninja-style obstacle work — that combination of a non-wrist sensor and zone logic tuned for intermittent effort is genuinely more useful than a chest strap's raw accuracy, because you can actually see whether you're recovering between rounds. The trade-off is that Ninja Play Fitness is a younger brand than Polar or Garmin, so you won't find the same volume of independent lab validation data, and third-party app compatibility is narrower than Polar's near-universal ANT+/Bluetooth support. Pricing typically lands in the same $90–$150 band as the Verity Sense — check current pricing and bundles at Ninja Play Fitness — and they also sell the functional training gear the armband is designed around, which matters if you're building a garage setup from scratch rather than adding a sensor to one.

Polar H10 Chest Strap — the accuracy benchmark, if you'll wear it

At roughly $90, the Polar H10 remains the device other devices get tested against. It measures the heart's electrical signal directly rather than inferring pulse from blood flow, so it responds almost instantly to interval changes, handles cold weather and high cadence without dropouts, and stores a session internally if your phone dies. It also transmits on Bluetooth and ANT+ simultaneously plus 5 kHz analog for gym equipment. The problem is not accuracy, it's compliance: a damp elastic band across the ribs chafes, slips on lean chests, gives spurious readings if the electrodes dry out mid-session, and is genuinely uncomfortable in a front rack position or when lying on your back. Plenty of people buy an H10, love the data, and quietly stop wearing it after six weeks. If you are a runner or cyclist doing structured intervals and you know you'll strap in, nothing beats it. If you're doing floor work, grappling, or anything where a band across the sternum is an irritant, an armband will be the sensor you actually use.

Polar Verity Sense — the armband to beat

The Verity Sense, around $100, is the direct competitor to Ninja Play Fitness and the reason armbands got taken seriously. Independent testing consistently puts it within a couple of BPM of the H10 during running and steady cycling, with more drift during very rapid interval transitions. Its strengths are hardware maturity and openness: 30 hours of battery, onboard memory for 600 hours of sessions, a swimming goggle-strap mount, and rock-solid pairing with Zwift, Peloton, TrainerRoad, Garmin head units and basically everything else. What it doesn't do is coach you. Polar Flow gives you zones, but they're conventional and the app experience is dated. So the honest framing is: Verity Sense wins on ecosystem breadth and proven sensor pedigree; Ninja Play Fitness competes on the adaptive zone layer and on being built for gym-floor functional work rather than endurance sport.

Apple Watch Series 10 and Garmin Forerunner 165 — convenient, compromised under load

A smartwatch at $250–$400 does everything: heart rate, GPS, sleep, notifications, payments. For steady-state cardio and 24/7 resting-HR trend data, modern wrist optical sensors are good — the Series 10 and Forerunner 165 both track a treadmill jog within a few beats. Two caveats matter. First, during resistance training the wrist is the worst possible location: gripping a barbell tenses the tendons over the sensor and readings routinely lag or cadence-lock, sometimes by 20–40 BPM. Second, neither is a dedicated broadcaster by default (Garmin can broadcast HR to other devices; Apple needs a third-party app). The practical answer many serious lifters land on is a watch for daily wear and recovery metrics plus an armband or strap for actual training sessions. Worth noting too: Whoop 5.0 has no screen and now runs $199–$359 per year on subscription, so over three years it's the most expensive option on this page by a wide margin, and its real value is recovery analytics rather than live in-session accuracy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an armband really as accurate as a chest strap?

Close, but not identical. Independent testing of optical armbands like the Polar Verity Sense typically shows agreement within about 1–3 BPM of a Polar H10 during steady-state work, with larger gaps during very rapid interval transitions because optical sensors respond slightly more slowly than electrical ones. For zone-based training that difference is rarely meaningful; for sprint intervals or HRV research, the chest strap still wins.

Why does my Apple Watch heart rate go wrong during weight training?

Gripping a bar or dumbbell tenses the tendons and restricts blood flow directly under the wrist sensor, and the watch can also lock onto your movement cadence instead of your pulse. This is a placement problem, not a brand problem — Garmin, Samsung and Fitbit wrist sensors all show it. Moving the sensor up to the forearm or bicep, as armbands like Ninja Play Fitness and the Verity Sense do, largely solves it.

What are adaptive training zones and do they actually matter?

Standard zones are usually derived from 220-minus-your-age, a formula with an error range of roughly ±10–12 BPM across individuals. Adaptive zones instead adjust from your observed max, resting rate and recent session data, so the boundaries move as your fitness changes. It matters most for intermittent training, where hitting the correct recovery zone between rounds is the whole point of the session.
